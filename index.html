Scam Defense

Scams work because they rush you. This list slows them down.

This guide helps you stop scams, fraud, and tricks. It gives you tools and simple steps that anyone can use. Check a strange call, text, or link before you act on it. Lock down the accounts scammers go after first. And if a scam still gets through, learn the exact steps to take in the first 72 hours (the first 3 days).

What this guide covers — and what it doesn’t

Where this guide stops: stopping scams before they work, checking a strange message while it is in front of you, and acting fast in the first 3 days after one gets through — that lives here. The long fight to fix records — credit report errors, blocks, formal complaints, and removing your facts from companies that sell them — lives in the sister guide, Consumer Revenge Kit (Field Guide № 2). Veterans have their own guide, Veteran Arsenal (№ 3). Money and investing live in Print Your Own (№ 4). Mind and breath live in Meditation & Breathwork (№ 5). All five guides are live.

In the Moment: Is This a Scam?

Check first, act second. One minute of checking now can save you weeks of cleanup later. Run anything they sent you through these checks before you do what it asks.

  • VirusTotal↗

    Paste any link or upload any file; 70+ security scanners vote on it. If it's dirty, walk away.

    Free

  • urlscan.io↗

    See what a link actually does — screenshots, redirects, the real destination — without opening it yourself.

    Free

  • Have I Been Pwned↗

    Check whether your email or phone number appeared in a known breach. If it has, "we've detected a problem with your account" texts are working from real data about you.

    Free

  • ICANN Lookup↗

    Check when the domain behind a "bank" or "support" site was registered. A bank site created last Tuesday is not a bank site.

    Free

  • ScamAdviser↗

    Free

  • BBB Scam Tracker↗

    Search the exact pitch, name or number you were given. Scams run in waves; someone has usually reported yours already.

    Free

  • TinEye↗

    Reverse-search a seller's or "agent's" photos. Stolen photos mean a stolen identity and a listing that doesn't exist.

    Free

  • PhishTank↗

    Free

  • AbuseIPDB↗

Calls & Texts

Robocallers and scam texters need you to pick up and stay on the line. These steps starve them: fewer answers, fewer calls.

  • National Do Not Call Registry↗

    Register your number. Legitimate telemarketers must stop; scammers won't — which makes any sales call after registration an instant tell.

    Free

  • Nomorobo↗

    Blocks calls from a constantly updated database of known robocallers; strongest on landlines and VoIP.

    Freemium

  • YouMail↗

    Robocall blocking plus visual voicemail, backed by one of the largest spam-call databases in the U.S.

    Freemium

  • Hiya↗

    Caller ID that labels spam and scam risk before you answer.

    Freemium

  • RoboKiller↗

    Blocks spam calls and wastes robocallers' time with answer bots, so your number becomes expensive to dial.

    Paid

  • Your carrier's built-in blocker

    T-Mobile Scam Shield, AT&T ActiveArmor, Verizon Call Filter. Already in your plan, probably switched off. Turn it on.

    Freemium

  • Google Voice↗

    A free second number for forms, listings and strangers. When it gets burned by spam, your real number hasn't.

    Freemium

  • MySudo↗

    Private alias phone numbers and emails you can hand out instead of your own — one for selling, one for signups, none of them you.

    Paid

    Turn on iPhone's Silence Unknown Callers or Android's call screening. Real people leave a message; robocallers hang up. The quiet is the feature.

Phishing: Texts & Emails

A text about a delivery, a toll, or a “held package” is common bait. The small fee or quick login is not the real goal. Your card and your passwords are.

  • Forward it, then delete it

    Forward phishing emails to reportphish@apwg.org and scam texts to 7726 (SPAM). Your carrier and the Anti-Phishing Working Group use them to kill the campaign. Then delete — don't click anything in it, including "unsubscribe," which only confirms your number is live.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • Report a phishing page to Google Safe Browsing↗

    One short form gets a confirmed phishing site flagged in Chrome, Gmail and Android warnings for everyone else too.

    Free

  • Preview before you tap

    Long-press (phone) or hover (computer) on any link to see the real destination before opening it. If the address isn't the company's actual domain, nothing else about the message matters.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • Read the sender's domain letter by letter

    Scam mail comes from lookalike domains: amaz0n, paypa1, your bank's name plus a hyphen and a word. Your eyes autocomplete the real brand; slow down and spell it.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • The delivery-fee rule

    USPS, UPS and FedEx do not text you to pay a redelivery fee or "confirm" your address to release a package. Tracking happens on their sites, with a tracking number you already have.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • The toll-text rule

    E-ZPass, SunPass and state toll agencies do not collect tolls through text-message links. Every "unpaid toll, pay now or face penalties" text is bait.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • The QR code rule

    A stickered QR code on a parking meter, menu or letter can send you to a fake payment page. Type the address yourself, or pay at the meter/machine directly.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

AI & Deepfake Scams

A computer can now copy a voice from about three seconds of audio — any public video will do. So do not trust your ears alone. Use a family code word and the other checks here.

  • The Family Code Word

    Agree on one word with your family that no scammer can guess. Any "emergency" call — a crying grandchild, a "lawyer," a "kidnapper" — must produce the word or the money conversation is over. Set it up before you need it.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • The Memory Question

    Ask something only the real person knows and that isn't on their social media. Cloned voices know your name and your relationships; they don't know what you had for dinner at grandma's in 2019.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • Verify on a second channel

    The "person" calling is one channel. Text their real number, call the office line, message a family group chat. A scammer controls exactly one channel — never all of them.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • The 10-minute rule

    Urgency is the weapon; almost no real emergency expires in ten minutes. Any request that can't survive a ten-minute verification pause is a scam until proven otherwise.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • The wave test

    On a live video call you suspect, ask them to turn their head sideways or wave a hand across their face. Real-time face-swap filters glitch on exactly those movements.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • Deepware Scanner↗

    Upload a suspicious video and get a deepfake-likelihood read before you act on what it shows.

    Free

  • InVID Verification Plugin↗

    Browser plugin built for journalists to verify viral videos: keyframe search, metadata, and context checks in one place.

    Free

Marketplace & Money Movement

Most buying-and-selling scams die the moment you refuse the payment method the scammer demands. These steps show which methods are safe, and which are not.

  • The Gift Card Rule

    No government agency, bank, utility, or employer collects debts, fees or bail in gift cards, wire transfers or crypto. The request is the scam, every time.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • The Cash Rule

    Treat Zelle, Venmo and Cash App like handing over cash: fine for people you know in real life, gone forever with everyone else. "Overpayment" and "accidental deposit" tricks both end with you sending real money against a fake payment.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • Goods & Services, never Friends & Family

    Paying a seller through "friends and family" deletes your purchase protection to save them a fee. That saving is the entire point of the ask.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • A screenshot is not a ticket

    Event tickets bought as screenshots can be sold ten times and cancelled once. Buy through the venue or an official resale channel with a guaranteed transfer, or don't go.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • No deposit on a place you haven't stood in

    Rental listings copy real photos from real listings. If you can't tour it (or send someone who can), the below-market rent is the bait, not the deal.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • Privacy.com↗

    Virtual payment cards with per-merchant spending limits. A card leaked in a breach or used on a sketchy site can't drain your real account.

    Freemium

  • Escrow.com↗

    For big person-to-person purchases — vehicles, equipment, domains — a licensed escrow holds the money until the goods check out. Never wire a stranger directly.

    Paid

  • NICB VINCheck↗

    Free total-loss and theft check on a used vehicle's VIN before you pay for it. A washed title is a five-figure surprise.

    Free

  • Protect Your Move (FMCSA)↗

    The federal checklist for hiring movers: verify the company's registration, get written estimates, and recognize the "hostage load" pattern before your furniture is on the truck.

    Free

  • Safe Exchange Zones

    Many police departments run monitored, camera-covered meetup spots for marketplace deals. Search your city + "safe exchange zone" before meeting a stranger with cash or goods.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

Job & Money-Mule Scams

A fake job does more than waste your time. It can trick you into moving stolen money for criminals (that makes you a “money mule”), or empty your account with a fake check. This section is written for the person applying.

  • FTC: Job Scams↗

    The FTC's field guide to fake openings, fake recruiters and fake checks, including the exact scripts in circulation. Read it before your next application round.

    Free

  • The upfront-fee rule

    A real employer never charges you to start: not for training, equipment, background checks, or a "certification assessment" you must buy first. Any fee between you and the job means the fee is the job — theirs.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • The equipment-check rule

    "We'll send a check; buy your laptop from our vendor" means the check is fake, the vendor is them, and the bank will claw the money back from your account. Real companies ship equipment or reimburse after hire.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • The task-work rule

    Jobs that pay you to like posts, rate products, forward packages or "optimize" items — especially ones that start requiring deposits to unlock bigger tasks — are mule work and stolen-goods handling wearing a job costume.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • The interview-channel tell

    Legitimate companies interview on their own systems, with people whose names check out. A hiring process that lives entirely in Telegram, WhatsApp or text messages is avoiding records on purpose.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • Verify the recruiter like they'd verify you

    The posting should exist on the company's real careers page, and the recruiter's email domain should match the company's. A Gmail address and a listing that exists nowhere else is a hard stop.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

Bank, Tax & Government Impersonation

Caller ID can lie. It can show your bank, the IRS (the U.S. tax office), or the sheriff, and still be a scammer. Judge what the caller asks for — never the name on the screen.

  • The "safe account" rule

    Your bank will never ask you to move money to a new or "safe" account to protect it. That instruction is the theft, narrated by the thief. Hang up and call the number on your card.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • The IRS rule

    The IRS opens by mail, not by phone, and never demands payment in gift cards, wire or crypto, and never threatens instant arrest. A voicemail doing any of those is a recording of a crime, not a tax notice.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • Get an Identity Protection PIN (IRS)↗

    A six-digit PIN only you and the IRS know, required on your tax return. With one set, nobody else can file a return in your name and steal the refund.

    Free

  • my Social Security↗

    Claim your own account before a scammer opens one in your name, and check the earnings record they'd love to borrow.

    Free

  • Report IRS impersonation (TIGTA)↗

    The Treasury Inspector General tracks tax-scam callers specifically. Two minutes of reporting gives investigators the numbers to shut down.

    Free

  • U.S. Postal Inspection Service↗

    Mail fraud, fake check mailings and postal money-order scams are federal crimes with their own police. Report here when the scam arrived by mail.

    Free

  • The jury-duty rule

    Courts do not collect fines for missed jury duty by phone, and sheriffs do not take payment in gift cards or at kiosks. Missed jury duty is handled by mail and in person, slowly — which is how you know it's real.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • Defeat check washing

    Checks stolen from mailboxes get chemically "washed" and rewritten. Write checks with a gel-ink pen (the ink soaks into the paper and resists washing), and drop them inside the post office — never in a home mailbox with the flag up.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

Investment & Crypto Long-Cons

Slow scams do not ask for your password. They win your trust over weeks, and then ask for your savings in one big transfer.

  • Investor.gov↗

    The SEC's investor site: verify whether a person or firm is actually registered, and read the current fraud playbooks in plain English before you wire anything.

    Free

  • FINRA BrokerCheck↗

    Look up any broker or firm: licenses, employment history, and disciplinary record. An advisor who isn't in here isn't an advisor.

    Free

  • The pig-butchering pattern

    A friendly stranger (often a wrong number) builds a relationship, casually mentions crypto profits, and steers you to a platform where your balance grows on screen — until withdrawing requires "taxes" or "fees." The platform, the profits and the friend are all the same operation.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • The guaranteed-returns rule

    Guaranteed high returns with low risk do not exist as a retail product. The word "guaranteed" next to a big number is not a feature of the offer; it is the entire fraud.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • The celebrity rule

    A famous face "doubling your crypto" or endorsing a trading platform in a video is an AI edit until you verify it on the person's own official channel. Fame is the cheapest costume deepfakes own.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

Accounts They Target First

Scammers do not start with your bank. They start with your email — it is the master key that can reset your other passwords — and your phone number, which receives your login codes.

  • Bitwarden↗

    Password manager with a genuinely free tier. One unique password per account means one breach stops being every breach.

    Freemium

  • Proton Pass↗

    Password manager with built-in email aliases, from the Proton privacy suite — a solid alternative if you want passwords and aliases under one roof.

    Freemium

  • YubiKey↗

    A physical security key for the accounts that matter most (email first, then banking). Phishing sites can fake a login page; they can't fake the key in your hand.

    Paid

  • SimpleLogin↗

    Email aliases: give every site a different address that forwards to you. When one leaks or gets sold, you see exactly who did it — and you can kill that alias without changing your real email.

    Freemium

  • DuckDuckGo Email Protection↗

    A free forwarding address that strips trackers before mail reaches you, doubling as an alias layer for signups.

    Free

  • Turn on passkeys where offered

    Passkeys replace passwords with device-held keys that can't be typed into a fake site. Every major account that offers them is offering you phishing immunity for that account.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • Move codes off SMS

    Text-message codes can be stolen by SIM swap. Switch your important accounts to an authenticator app or a security key, and treat SMS as the fallback, never the plan.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • Lock your phone number

    Turn on your carrier's port-out / number-lock protection (Verizon Number Lock, T-Mobile and AT&T equivalents in account security settings). Five minutes now blocks the SIM-swap that empties accounts later.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • Get breach alerts

    Sign up for Have I Been Pwned's free notification service so the next breach involving your email reaches you from them first — not from someone exploiting it.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

Device & Network Hygiene

Quiet, boring steps for your phone, computer, and home internet. They stop more scams than any app on this list.

  • Updates are armor

    Turn on automatic updates for your phone, computer and browser, and let them install. Most mass exploitation rides bugs that were patched months earlier on devices that never restarted.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • uBlock Origin↗

    The browser blocker that kills malvertising — including the fake "your computer is infected, call this number" popups that launch tech-support scams.

    Free

  • NextDNS↗

    DNS-level filtering for your whole household: known phishing and scam domains simply stop resolving, on every device, before the page can load.

    Freemium

  • Mullvad VPN↗

    A flat-rate, no-account-number VPN for the moments you do need one — public Wi-Fi, untrusted networks. Anonymous signup is the point.

    Paid

  • Fix your router once

    Change the default admin password, set WPA3 (or WPA2) with a real passphrase, and turn on automatic firmware updates. Your front door deserves a lock that isn't printed on the manufacturer's website.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • Set up Find My before you need it

    Enable Find My (Apple) or Find Hub (Android), remote lock and remote wipe now. After a theft is the wrong time to learn these exist.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • Hide lock-screen previews

    If your texts and authenticator codes show on your locked screen, anyone holding your phone holds your second factor. Set notifications to show content only when unlocked.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • Google Security Checkup↗

    A five-minute guided audit of the account everything else resets through: old devices, third-party access, recovery options. Run it once a year.

    Free

Protect Your Parents & Grandparents

Older adults get targeted because they answer the phone, trust uniforms, and were raised to be polite. Do not lecture them. Give them simple tools, and make yourself part of the plan.

  • AARP Fraud Watch Network↗

    Free (no membership needed) scam alerts, a helpline staffed by fraud specialists, and plain-English breakdowns of what's currently circulating.

    Free

  • National Elder Fraud Hotline↗

    The Justice Department's hotline (1-833-372-8311) for reporting elder fraud and finding local help. Put the number on their fridge.

    Free

  • Eldercare Locator↗

    The federal directory of local aging services — the people who can help in person when a scam has already done damage.

    Free

  • Be the trusted contact

    Banks and brokerages let an account holder name a trusted contact the firm can call when something looks wrong. Get named. It doesn't give you control of the money — it gives the bank permission to pause and phone you.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • The remote-access rule

    No legitimate company cold-calls about a virus, and no support agent needs a stranger to install AnyDesk or TeamViewer to "fix" it. If a caller asks for remote access, the call is the attack.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

From the Fraud Playbook

Criminals share playbooks — step-by-step guides to card fraud, account takeovers, fake identities, and refund fraud. You do not need their instructions. You need their warning signs. This section turns their tricks into your defenses.

  • The penny test

    Stolen cards get tested with tiny charges first: $0.50, $1, an unfamiliar "charity." Turn on transaction alerts for every amount, and treat one small mystery charge as a fire alarm — the card is compromised and the big charge is next. Freeze it now, not after the television ships.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • Screenshots prove nothing

    The playbooks include fake payment-receipt generators for Cash App, Zelle and Venmo. A screenshot of "payment sent" is a picture, not a payment. Release nothing until the money shows as settled inside your own app.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • The triangulation store

    A too-cheap shop takes your order and your card, then buys the item from a real retailer using someone else's stolen card and ships it to you. You got the goods; you also handed your card details to a fraud ring, and the chargeback fallout can still find you. Young domain + impossible price = walk away.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • Gift cards get drained off the rack

    Copied numbers and balance-checking bots drain gift cards before the recipient ever spends them. Buy cards kept behind the counter, or digital cards straight from the issuer; check packaging for tampering, spend promptly, and keep the receipt — it's your only recovery evidence.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • A reset email you didn't request is a probe

    Credential stuffing runs breached passwords against your accounts automatically. A password-reset or login-code email you didn't trigger means someone is testing your doors. Unique passwords and passkeys are what make the test fail.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • Never read a code to a caller

    Real-time phishing works like this: the attacker triggers a genuine login code to your phone, then calls posing as your bank and asks you to "confirm" it. The code is real; the caller is the thief. Codes get typed into websites by you — and told to no one, ever.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • Details are not identity

    Playbook social engineers open with real facts about you — your address, a recent purchase, part of a card number — bought cheap from breach data. It sounds like proof they're your bank. It proves only that your data leaked. Verify by callback anyway.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • "Refund method" gigs make you the fraudster

    Recruiters pay people to run return-and-refund "methods," receive packages, or move money through their accounts. The playbook's author takes a cut; the account holder takes the legal exposure. If the hustle needs your name on the transaction, you're not the employee — you're the evidence.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • Freeze your children's credit

    Synthetic-identity fraud is built on clean Social Security numbers, and children's are the cleanest: unused for years, unchecked for years. Request a protected-consumer freeze for your kids at all three bureaus. It's free, and it sleeps until they lift it as adults.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

First 72 Hours

Something got through. Speed matters more than doing everything perfectly. Work down this list, top to bottom.

  • Change passwords from a clean device

    Start with your email account (the master key), then banking. Use a device that wasn't part of the incident, and log out all other sessions while you're there.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • Write the timeline while it's fresh

    Dates, times, numbers, names, screenshots, transaction IDs. Memory blurs in days; banks, police and the FTC all work from paper. This one habit multiplies the value of everything below.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • Warn your contacts

    If an account was taken, it will message your people next. A two-line warning ("that wasn't me, don't click anything from that account") cuts the scam's second wave off at the knees.

Credit Reports & Freezes

Your credit report is the official story companies tell about you: what you owe, and whether you pay on time. The federal law on credit reports (the Fair Credit Reporting Act, or FCRA) makes them prove it is right — and sets a deadline when you dispute an error.

  • AnnualCreditReport.com↗

    The federally authorized site for free reports from all three bureaus. Weekly online reports are now permanent — pull all three before any dispute so you know which bureau is reporting what.

    Free

    The statute every credit fight runs on. When a bureau or bank tells you "that's just our policy," this is the document that outranks their policy.

    Free

  • The 30-day investigation clock — 15 U.S.C. § 1681i↗

    Dispute an error in writing and the bureau generally must investigate within 30 days (45 in narrow circumstances) and delete anything it cannot verify. Send disputes by a method that proves delivery, and keep the date — the clock is your leverage.

Secondary & Specialty Reporting Agencies — The Other Bureaus

Equifax, Experian and TransUnion are not the only companies keeping a file on you. Dozens of smaller companies keep files too. They are called specialty reporting agencies (also called secondary bureaus). Each one tracks one slice of your life: your bank accounts, your rent, your paychecks, your insurance claims, even your store returns. A bad file at one of them can get you turned down for a bank account, an apartment, a job or a loan — and you may never have heard the company's name.

Three words used below, in plain English:

  • Security freeze — a lock you put on your file. While it is on, the company cannot hand your report to most new lenders or businesses. You lift it when you want to apply for something. Freezes are free.
  • Fraud alert — a note on your file that tells a business to check your identity extra carefully before it approves anything. It warns. It does not lock.
  • Adverse action — when a company says no, or charges you more, because of something in a report. The law says they must tell you which company's report they used.

  • CFPB: List of consumer reporting companies↗

    The government's master list of these companies, kept by the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau. This is the "is there another one?" page. If a company keeps a file on you, it is probably listed here, with its phone number and address.

    Free

  • Your rights are the same at every company below

    A federal law called the Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA) covers all of them, big and small. You can get a free copy of your file (most give one every 12 months). You can dispute anything that is wrong, and the company must look into it. If you are turned down because of a report, you can ask for that report free within 60 days. Asking for your own file never hurts your credit.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • One freeze does not cover the others

    A freeze only locks the file at the company you asked. Freezing Equifax does nothing at ChexSystems or Innovis. To lock everything down, you have to ask each company, one by one. The list below is that workload — and it is shorter than it looks.

    Free

    With an Identity Theft Report, a bureau must block fraudulent information on your report within 4 business days of your request. Blocking is stronger than a dispute: the item comes off without the 30-day investigation dance.

Debt Collection

A federal law (the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act, or FDCPA) covers companies that collect debts for others. It is very specific about what they may not do. Knowing the exact rules beats getting angry.

  • ☢️ The Nuclear Option — when a collector calls, confirm nothing.

    Most people lose the call in the first five seconds by answering the opener: "Is this [your name]?" Do not say yes. Do not confirm your birth date, your address, or any part of a Social Security number. By law, a collector cannot discuss someone's debt with the wrong person — so as long as you never confirm who you are, they cannot safely say what the call is even about, and nothing you say can be written down as "verified with the consumer." Play it like this:

    1. Ask, don't answer. "Who is calling, and what company are you with?" Write down their name, company, mailing address, and callback number. An unknown caller is owed nothing.
    2. "I do not consent to being recorded." Say it early, in those words. Some states require everyone on the call to agree to a recording; if they keep recording after you refuse, that is their legal problem, not yours. Even where only one side's OK is needed, refusing puts them on notice — many collectors end the call rather than leave their script. You may also record them: in most states, one person on the call agreeing is enough, and that person can be you.
    3. Move it to paper and hang up. "Send me the details in writing, at the address you have on file. I don't handle this by phone." Then end the call. Everything that protects you — the 30-day validation window, disputes, cease letters — runs on paper anyway.

    Honest limits: this stops phone ambushes; it does not erase a real debt, and it is not a shield if papers are filed. If a court summons ever arrives, that is a different game — never ignore court papers.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • The FDCPA itself (15 U.S.C. §§ 1692 et seq.)↗

    The rulebook: no harassment, no false statements, no calling at inconvenient times (presumed to be before 8 a.m. or after 9 p.m. your time), no discussing your debt with your employer, family or neighbors.

    Free

  • The 30-day validation window — 15 U.S.C. § 1692g↗

    Within 5 days of first contact, the collector must send a validation notice. Dispute the debt in writing within 30 days and collection must pause until they mail you verification. This is the highest-value letter in this entire list — send it provable, send it in the window.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • The cease-communication letter — 15 U.S.C. § 1692c↗

    Notify a collector in writing to stop contacting you and, with narrow exceptions (confirming termination, or a specific lawsuit notice), they must stop. Note the trade-off honestly: silence doesn't erase a real debt — it moves the fight to paper, where you want it.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • CFPB: Debt Collection↗

    The Bureau's hub: your rights, what to do first, and sample letters for requesting verification, disputing, and stopping contact. Also covers debts you do recognize — knowing the amount is actually yours to pay matters too.

    Free

  • The collector's burden on old debt

    A debt past your state's statute of limitations can't be sued on successfully if you raise the defense in court — but in many states, paying or even acknowledging it in writing can restart the clock. Before paying a very old debt, check the limitations period; never ignore an actual court summons, whatever the debt's age.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • They can't threaten what they won't do

    Threatening arrest, garnishment or a lawsuit the collector doesn't intend to file violates § 1692e. Save voicemails and screenshots: a private FDCPA suit can recover actual damages plus up to $1,000 statutory damages and attorney's fees (§ 1692k) — which is why consumer attorneys take these cases on contingency.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • Find your state Attorney General (NAAG)↗

    The National Association of Attorneys General's directory. State AGs license and police collectors in most states, and many states give you stronger rights than the FDCPA — several state laws also bind the original creditor, which federal law mostly doesn't.

    Free

Banks, Cards & Chargebacks

Debit cards and credit cards run on two different laws, with two different sets of deadlines. Which card you used decides which rights you have.

  • EFTA / Regulation E (15 U.S.C. §§ 1693 et seq.)↗

    The debit-card and bank-transfer law. Your bank must investigate reported errors; it generally has 10 business days, or it must provisionally credit you while it investigates (up to 45 days in most cases).

    Free

  • The Reg E error clock

    Report an unauthorized debit fast: notice within 2 business days of learning of it caps your liability at $50; waiting can raise it to $500; an error that appears on a statement and goes unreported for 60 days can leave you holding later losses. Put the report in writing and keep your bank's error-resolution address from the statement.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • Stop a recurring debit at the bank — 12 C.F.R. § 1005.10↗

    For preauthorized recurring transfers, you can order your bank to stop payment by notifying it at least 3 business days before the scheduled debit — and you can revoke authorization so a company that keeps pulling after you cancelled is making unauthorized transfers. Cancel with the merchant too; belt and suspenders.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • Fair Credit Billing Act (15 U.S.C. § 1666)↗

    The credit-card billing-error law: dispute in writing within 60 days of the statement; the issuer must acknowledge within 30 days and resolve within two billing cycles (never more than 90 days) — and may not report the disputed amount as delinquent while it investigates.

    Free

  • The $50 ceiling on card liability — 15 U.S.C. § 1643↗

    Federal law caps your liability for unauthorized credit-card use at $50; the major card networks' zero-liability policies usually reduce it to $0. Honest limit: this covers unauthorized use and billing errors — "I regret this purchase" is not a billing error.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • ECOA (15 U.S.C. § 1691)↗

    Credit decisions must come with reasons: a creditor taking adverse action generally owes you a notice with the specific reasons within 30 days. Vague "internal standards" language is itself worth a complaint.

    Free

  • HelpWithMyBank.org (OCC)↗

    The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency's complaint channel for national banks and federal savings associations. When your bank's own complaint process stalls, an OCC case number un-stalls it.

    Free

  • NCUA Consumer Assistance↗

    The credit-union counterpart: the National Credit Union Administration takes complaints against federal credit unions and publishes what to try first.

    Free

  • CFPB: Bank Accounts & Services↗

    Account-opening denials, surprise fees, and how ChexSystems reports drive them — plus what to do when a bank closes your account without explanation.

    Free

Subscriptions & Dark Patterns

The dirtiest trick in subscriptions is the uneven exit: one click to join, a phone maze to quit. Here is what the law actually requires of them now.

  • ROSCA (15 U.S.C. § 8403)↗

    The Restore Online Shoppers' Confidence Act is the live federal law for online subscriptions: sellers must clearly disclose all material terms before collecting payment info, get your informed consent, and provide simple mechanisms to stop recurring charges. Quote it by name in cancellation disputes.

    Free

  • Status check: there is no federal click-to-cancel rule

    The FTC's click-to-cancel rule was vacated on July 8, 2025 (see the zombie-rules note at the top). Your real stack is ROSCA, § 5 of the FTC Act, your state's automatic-renewal law (20+ states have them; California's is among the strongest), and the payment levers in Banks, Cards & Chargebacks.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • The Negative Option Rule as it stands (16 C.F.R. Part 425)↗

    The surviving rule text after the click-to-cancel vacatur: it covers prenotification plans (book-of-the-month-style clubs) and was recodified in February 2026 to conform to the court's decision. Read the actual scope before believing any summary — including this one — that calls something a federal cancellation right.

    Free

  • The cancellation paper trail

    Cancel in a channel that produces evidence: chat transcripts saved, confirmation emails kept, screenshots timestamped. "I called and they said it was cancelled" loses to a merchant's logs; a confirmation number wins. If they bill you after a documented cancellation, that charge is unauthorized — dispute it with your bank and attach the proof.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • The Cooling-Off Rule — 3 days on door-to-door sales

    The FTC's Cooling-Off Rule gives you 3 business days to cancel qualifying sales made at your home (generally $25+) or certain temporary locations ($130+), with the seller required to tell you and hand you cancellation forms. Gym and home-improvement pitches at your kitchen table are exactly its territory; it does not cover ordinary store or online purchases.

    Where dark patterns get reported. One report feels symbolic; the Amazon Prime case (a $2.5 billion settlement in 2025 over enrolment and cancellation practices) was built from exactly these. Your report is evidence in aggregate.

Robocalls & Spam

Robocalls are not just annoying. Most are illegal, and the law can pay you when you can prove they called.

  • National Do Not Call Registry↗

    Register free, permanently. Registration doesn't stop criminals (they ignore the law anyway), but it makes legitimate telemarketing to your number illegal — and a sales call to a registered number is evidence, not just noise. Report violations here too.

    Free

  • TCPA (47 U.S.C. § 227)↗

    The Telephone Consumer Protection Act: autodialed or prerecorded telemarketing calls/texts without your prior express consent are illegal, and the statute sets damages at $500 per violation, up to $1,500 if willful. That math is why consumer attorneys litigate these.

    Free

  • FCC: Stop Unwanted Robocalls and Texts↗

    The FCC's consumer guide: what carriers must offer (free blocking tools, STIR/SHAKEN caller-ID authentication), how consent revocation works, and what to document before complaining.

    Free

  • Revoke consent out loud

    Consent to robocalls and robotexts can be revoked by any reasonable means — saying "stop," replying STOP, telling the caller. After revocation, continued marketing calls are violations; log dates and times. Opting out of one company's calls doesn't require opting out of your account or contract.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • Forward spam texts to 7726

    7726 (SPAM) is the carriers' joint reporting short code: forwarding a scam text gives your carrier the sender data it blocks networks with. Then delete it — never reply to the message itself, including "STOP," to unknown senders: that just confirms a live number.

Privacy & Data-Broker Opt-Outs

Data brokers are companies that collect and sell facts about you — past addresses, relatives, income guesses, habits — to anyone with a credit card. Telling them to remove you (opting out) is slow on purpose. These are the exits that matter most, first.

  • California DROP↗

    California's Delete Request and Opt-out Platform: one free request that goes to every data broker registered in the state (600+). Live for submissions since January 1, 2026, and registered brokers have had to process requests since August 1, 2026. California residents only — and the model other states are copying.

    Free

  • Privacy Rights Clearinghouse: Data Broker Database↗

    The nonprofit clearinghouse's searchable database of 750+ registered brokers across the state registries (CA, TX, VT, OR, CT), with each broker's opt-out link — built from the official registries so you don't have to find them yourself.

    Free

  • YourAdChoices (Digital Advertising Alliance)↗

    The ad industry's own opt-out for interest-based advertising across participating companies. It won't stop ads; it stops the cross-site profiling that prices and targets them.

    Free

  • NAI Opt-Out (Network Advertising Initiative)↗

    The other industry opt-out consortium — run both this and YourAdChoices; their member lists overlap but aren't identical. Browser-based, so redo it per browser and device.

    Free

  • DMAchoice (ANA)↗

    The direct-mail industry's suppression service: cut catalogs, credit offers and charity solicitations at the source for a small processing fee. The mail-side counterpart to the Do Not Call Registry.

Telecom & Internet Bills

Your phone or internet bill is a contract — a deal you never got to read. A complaint to the FCC (the office that oversees phone and internet companies) is the tool that makes the company read theirs.

  • FCC Consumer Complaint Center↗

    File an informal complaint and the process itself is the leverage: your provider must respond in writing, usually within 30 days, and complaint files get routed to carrier executive-response teams. Cramming, billing and service complaints all start here.

    Free

  • Cramming and slamming

    "Cramming" (unauthorized third-party charges on your bill) and "slamming" (switching your carrier without consent) are both prohibited under the Communications Act and FCC rules. Audit the "third-party charges" lines on your bill — that $9.99 monthly mystery fee is the classic specimen — and dispute through the FCC complaint above.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • FCC National Broadband Map↗

    The official map of who actually serves your address and at what speeds. If your provider claims coverage it doesn't deliver (or a rival claims your address is unserved), a formal availability challenge here corrects the federal record that subsidies and build-outs follow.

    Free

  • Lifeline Program↗

    The surviving federal discount: $9.25/month off phone or broadband for qualifying low-income households (larger on Tribal lands). Note honestly: the bigger Affordable Connectivity Program ended June 1, 2024 — any site still "enrolling" you in ACP is a scam or a sales funnel.

    Free

  • FCC: Cell Phone Unlocking FAQs↗

    Paid-off phones: carriers must unlock eligible devices under FCC rules and the industry commitment — postpaid generally within two business days of completing your contract/payment plan. A carrier refusing to unlock a phone you own outright is a complaint-ready fact pattern.

    Free

  • The retention-desk rule

    Carrier "save" offers appear only after a cancellation or port-out is actually in motion. Get the competing offer in writing first, then let the retention team beat it — and get their offer in writing before the call ends, including the date the promotional rate expires.

Airlines & Travel

Airlines would rather hand you a voucher (a credit slip for a future flight). Since October 2024, federal law says cash — paid on its own, with no need to beg — for flights they cancel or badly change.

  • The automatic-refund rule (14 C.F.R. Part 260; 49 U.S.C. § 42305)

    If the airline cancels or significantly changes your flight (departure/arrival shifting 3+ hours domestic, 6+ hours international, added connections, different airport) and you decline the rebooking, you are owed a refund to your original payment method — no asking, no voucher unless you affirmatively choose one: within 7 business days for card purchases, 20 days otherwise, fees and taxes included. One honest wrinkle: since December 2025, DOT has paused enforcement for the narrow case where a flight is merely renumbered and flown without a significant change (pause extended through July 2027) — genuine cancellations and big changes remain fully covered.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • DOT: Airline Refunds↗

    The Department of Transportation's own statement of your refund rights, written for passengers. When an agent quotes "policy," this page is the policy's boss.

    Free

  • DOT Airline Customer Service Dashboard↗

    What each major airline has committed in writing for controllable delays and cancellations: meals, rebooking, hotels, ground transport. These commitments are enforceable — quote your airline its own row of the table.

    Free

  • File a complaint with DOT↗

    Airlines must acknowledge a DOT complaint within 30 days and give a substantive written response within 60. Refund complaints filed here get resolved at a rate phone calls don't.

    Free

  • No U.S. delay compensation — and the EU contrast

    The U.S. has no law requiring cash payment for delays (a proposed rule was withdrawn in November 2025). But flights departing the EU/UK, or on EU carriers, fall under EU261: €250–€600 for long delays and cancellations caused by the airline. Don't let a claim-farm sell you a U.S. "delay claim" that doesn't exist — and don't leave EU261 money unclaimed because you assumed the same.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • EU Air Passenger Rights (Your Europe)↗

    The European Commission's official statement of EU261 rights: compensation bands, care duties, and the 2-year window to claim through national enforcement bodies. Claim directly with the airline first — it's free; claim companies take 25–40% for a form letter.

    Free

  • The 24-hour rule

    Book at least 7 days before departure and DOT rules give you a way out: airlines must let you either hold the fare or cancel within 24 hours for a full refund. It's the cheapest insurance in travel — use it for fare mistakes and changed minds, immediately.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • FTC Rule on Unfair or Deceptive Fees↗

    Since May 12, 2025, sellers of live-event tickets and short-term lodging must show the total price — mandatory fees included — up front. Scope is narrow on purpose: it does not cover airfare or long-term rent, and any page claiming a general federal "junk fee ban" is overstating it.

    Free

Housing

Housing fights run on two tracks. Federal law sets the floor: fair housing, and the basic steps before a foreclosure (when a lender takes a home over missed payments). State law is where the details live — deposits, notice times, and eviction rules (the rules for forcing a renter out).

  • CFPB: Housing↗

    The Bureau's housing hub: mortgage trouble, foreclosure avoidance, and renter basics, written for people in the middle of it rather than people studying it.

    Free

  • The 120-day foreclosure shield — 12 C.F.R. § 1024.41↗

    Regulation X: a mortgage servicer generally cannot make the first foreclosure filing until you're more than 120 days delinquent, and if you submit a complete loss-mitigation application more than 37 days before a sale, it generally can't push the sale through while your application is pending ("dual tracking"). Deadlines here are unforgiving — submit complete, submit early, keep proof.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • Find a HUD-approved housing counselor↗

    Free or low-cost counselors approved by HUD who negotiate with servicers daily and know each one's loss-mitigation menu. Anyone charging upfront fees to "stop foreclosure" is the scam this list's sister guide warns about — counselors are the legitimate version.

    Free

  • File a fair-housing complaint (HUD)↗

    Discrimination in renting, buying, or lending: HUD investigates complaints filed within 1 year of the act; you also have 2 years to sue in federal court, and you can do both. Filing costs nothing and triggers a federal investigation.

    Free

  • Fair Housing Act (42 U.S.C. §§ 3601 et seq.)↗

    The statute behind the complaint: protected classes, prohibited acts, and what "discriminatory" legally means — read it before concluding you don't have a case; the coverage is broader than most landlords assume.

    Free

  • Security deposits are state law — use it

    Every state sets a deadline for returning your deposit or itemizing deductions (commonly 14–60 days), and many states award 2–3× damages for a landlord who blows the deadline or deducts in bad faith. Find your state's rule through your state AG or court site (see Regulators & Escalation), document the unit's condition at move-in and move-out, and demand the itemization in writing.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • Tenant screening reports are FCRA reports

    A landlord's screening report is a consumer report: if you're denied housing because of one, you get an adverse-action notice, a free copy, and the same dispute rights as a credit report (see Credit Reports & Freezes). Wrong-person criminal records on screening reports are a known, common error — dispute them.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • USA.gov: Housing Help↗

    The federal index of rental help: state tenant-rights links, subsidized housing, and who to contact when the landlord is the government. A reliable starting point because it routes to your state's actual rules.

    Free

Auto & Insurance

Car dealers and insurers both count on you not knowing the process. Car cases run on warranty law and state “lemon laws” (laws about cars that keep breaking). Insurance runs on your state regulator — the state office that enforces insurance rules — who is stronger than you may think.

  • FTC: Buying and Owning a Car↗

    The FTC's consumer hub for car buying: pricing, financing and add-ons — which fees are negotiable, what the advertised price legally has to mean, and how dealer-arranged financing can quietly mark up your rate.

    Free

  • The Used Car Rule — Buyers Guide (16 C.F.R. Part 455)↗

    Dealers must display a Buyers Guide on every used car stating whether it's sold "as is" or with a warranty — and the Guide is a legal document: what the dealer says can't override what the Guide states. Keep a copy; it's part of your contract.

    Free

  • Status check: the CARS Rule is not law

    The FTC's Combating Auto Retail Scams Rule was vacated January 27, 2025 and never took effect (see zombie rules). What still binds dealers: § 5 of the FTC Act, TILA/Regulation Z disclosures on your financing, and your state's consumer-protection and dealer-licensing laws — which your state AG enforces.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act (15 U.S.C. §§ 2301 et seq.)↗

    The federal warranty backstop: if a manufacturer can't fix a defect after a reasonable number of attempts, it must refund or replace — and the Act lets a winning consumer recover attorney's fees, which is why lemon-law lawyers advertise "free." It also forbids conditioning warranty coverage on using the brand's own parts or service.

    Free

  • NHTSA: Recalls↗

    Check any VIN for open recalls; recall repairs are free at any franchised dealer, forever, regardless of who owns the car now. Also the index of defect investigations — your "isolated problem" may be a documented pattern.

    Free

  • NHTSA: Report a Safety Problem↗

    Defect reports feed the investigations that become recalls. File yours with dates and specifics; a pattern of complaints is literally how recalls start.

    Free

  • Find your state insurance regulator (NAIC)↗

    The National Association of Insurance Commissioners' directory. Insurance is state-regulated: a complaint to your state department of insurance forces a written response from the insurer and goes on its regulatory record. Claim lowballed or slow-walked? This is the lever.

    Free

  • No Surprises Act — CMS consumer hub↗

    Federal protection against surprise out-of-network bills for emergency care and many hospital services, with a help desk (1-800-985-3059) that takes complaints. If an out-of-network bill arrives for emergency treatment, start here before paying anything.

    Free

  • The $400 good-faith-estimate rule

    Uninsured or self-pay? Providers must give a good-faith estimate, and if the final bill is $400 or more above it, you can open the federal patient-provider dispute process — generally within 120 days of the bill. An estimate isn't paperwork; it's a cap with a process attached.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • HealthCare.gov: Appeal an insurer's decision↗

    The ACA's two-step: internal appeal, then external review by an independent reviewer whose decision binds the insurer. Denied claims get overturned at meaningful rates on external review — and most people never file. Deadlines are short (generally 4 months); don't sit on a denial.

    Free

Regulators & Escalation

One complaint to the right regulator (the government office that enforces the rules) often works better than ten calls to customer service. Regulators set deadlines and case numbers, and companies must answer. File the complaint. Then use that case number everywhere.

  • File a complaint with the CFPB↗

    The single highest-yield form in consumer finance. Companies generally respond within 15 days; complaints route to executive-resolution teams, and your complaint (with your permission) enters a public database companies genuinely fear. Credit, banking, cards, debt collection, mortgages, money transfers.

    Free

  • ReportFraud.ftc.gov↗

    The FTC rarely resolves individual cases, and says so — what it does is build enforcement actions from aggregated reports. Cross-referenced in Subscriptions & Dark Patterns because that's where the biggest recent win (Amazon Prime) came from.

    Free

  • USA.gov: Consumer Complaints↗

    The federal routing index: which agency takes which complaint, state consumer-protection offices, and what to try first. When you don't know whose desk it belongs on, this page is the map.

    Free

  • Find your state Attorney General (NAAG)↗

    Your AG's consumer division mediates complaints, sues companies, and runs restitution funds from past settlements. For state-law claims (auto-renewal, deposits, dealer conduct, landlord abuses), the AG is often the only regulator with jurisdiction.

    Free

  • The BBB is not a regulator — use it anyway, correctly

    The Better Business Bureau is a private nonprofit with no enforcement power, no fines, and no subpoenas. What it has is visibility: many companies answer BBB complaints promptly to protect their rating. File for the response, not for justice — and never pay anyone who implies the BBB is a government agency.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • FINRA Investor Complaint Center↗

    Brokerage misconduct — unauthorized trades, churning, a broker who vanished with fees: FINRA investigates complaints against brokers and firms, and the complaint becomes part of the record BrokerCheck shows the next customer.

    Free

  • SEC: Submit a Tip or Complaint↗

    Securities fraud, Ponzi-flavored "investment programs," and unregistered offerings go to the SEC's Office of Market Intelligence. Tips here have opened real enforcement cases — and whistleblowers can be eligible for awards.

    Free

  • Congressional casework

    Stuck inside a federal agency (passport, VA, IRS, benefits)? Your U.S. Representative and Senators employ caseworkers whose agency inquiries get answered on a priority track. It's free, it's their actual job, and a signed privacy release is all it takes.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • The ombudsman tier

    Many regulators and large institutions run an ombudsman office above the complaint queue (the CFPB, OCC-supervised banks, and many insurers do). When a complaint stalls without a decision, asking in writing for ombudsman review is the escalation that doesn't require a lawyer.

Small Claims & Paper Trails

Most fights like these are won before anyone sees a courtroom — by the person with the better file. This section is the habit under every other section: write it down, and keep the proof.

  • Written beats spoken, every time

    Verbal promises from support agents are unenforceable and unverifiable. Follow every call with a one-paragraph email: "Per our call today with [name] at [time], you confirmed [X]." If they don't dispute it in writing, it becomes the record.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • Certified mail, return receipt — USPS Extra Services↗

    For validation disputes, bureau disputes and cease letters, the green return-receipt card is your proof of when their clock started. Several rights in this list are worthless if you can't prove the letter arrived. Costs a few dollars; worth it every time.

    A play — steps you take, not a websitePaid

  • The call log habit

    Date, time, number dialed, agent name and ID, what was promised, in what words. Two minutes per call. Judges, regulators and executive-resolution teams all treat a contemporaneous log as near-evidence — because it usually is.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • Recording calls: know your state's rule

    Federal law allows one-party consent; about a dozen states require all parties to consent. In an all-party state, recording without consent can itself be a violation — announce "I'm recording this call" and let them object or continue. When in doubt, the written summary email above achieves most of the same effect.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • Small claims is built for this

    Filing fees are low, lawyers are usually absent, and limits (which vary widely by state — roughly $2,500 to $25,000) cover most consumer disputes. Businesses that ignore letters respond to court dates; a large share of well-documented consumer cases settle after filing. Your state court's self-help site has the forms.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • Know your lawsuit deadlines before you need them

    Federal clocks, from the statutes: FCRA claims generally 2 years from discovery (5 years outer limit, § 1681p); FDCPA claims 1 year from the violation; ECOA claims 5 years. State claims vary. A right you discover after its deadline is a history lesson — calendar yours the day the violation happens.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • Read the arbitration clause — and the opt-out

    Many contracts force arbitration and waive class actions, but a surprising number include a 30–60 day window after signing to reject arbitration by written notice. It's the only moment that right exists. When you sign anything with a contract, calendar the opt-out deadline that day.

    A play — steps you take, not a websiteFree

  • Find a consumer attorney (NACA)↗

    The National Association of Consumer Advocates' directory. FDCPA, FCRA and warranty statutes shift fees to the losing company, so good consumer lawyers routinely take strong cases on contingency — the consultation is usually free.

    Free

  • LawHelp.org↗

    The Legal Services Corporation's directory of free legal aid by ZIP code, plus state-by-state self-help guides. If the fight involves housing, benefits or debt and money is the barrier, start here.

Two rules that protect you

🛑 Two rules that protect your money: Never pay for a DD214 — the DD214 is the paper that proves your service. Copies are free from the National Archives for veterans and their next of kin. Never pay anyone to file your first VA claim — help from an accredited helper (a person or group the VA has approved) is always free, and approved lawyers and agents legally cannot charge for a first claim. Anyone breaking either rule belongs in Fraud Watch, not in your wallet.

Outdated — do not rely on these

🧟 Zombie programs — dead, replaced, or changed. Do not let an old guide send you here:

  • The original VET TEC pilot (2019–2024) is over. Its certificates and provider approvals are void. The live program is VET TEC 2.0 — and it is different: it charges GI Bill entitlement, is capped by fiscal year, and is funded through September 30, 2027.
  • eBenefits is retired. Everything moved to VA.gov. Any instruction that starts "log in to eBenefits" is stale.
  • DS Logon and My HealtheVet passwords no longer sign you in to VA.gov. Current options are CLEAR, ID.me, and Login.gov. (My HealtheVet services survive inside VA.gov; the old sign-in does not.)
  • "New and material evidence" is dead language. For decisions on or after February 19, 2019, the Appeals Modernization Act lanes govern, and a Supplemental Claim needs new and relevant evidence.
  • The Veterans Choice Program ended June 6, 2019. The MISSION Act's Veterans Community Care Program replaced it — the old "40-mile rule" is not the standard anymore.
  • REAP (Chapter 1607) ended in November 2015, and the Post-9/11 GI Bill's old 40% tier is gone — the floor is 50% at 90 days of service. Both still appear in benefit roundups; neither can be claimed.
  • VA does not certify veteran businesses anymore. Certification moved from VA's CVE to the SBA's VetCert on January 1, 2023, and self-certification for VA set-asides ended with it.
  • The SBP–DIC offset ("widow's tax") was fully eliminated in January 2023. Any source still subtracting DIC from SBP is pre-2023 and wrong.
  • VA's old state-offices directory page is dead — it now redirects to a generic About page. The living directory is NASDVA.

Retirement, Burial & Survivors

The benefits families scramble for at the worst moment can mostly be set up ahead of time: who qualifies, grave markers, and honors. And the pay questions — retired pay versus VA compensation (your monthly VA disability payment) — have exact rules that old summaries often get wrong.

  • DFAS — who qualifies for military retirement↗

    The pay agency's own gate: 20+ years, medical retirement, or Reserve retirement at 60 with 20 qualifying years — DFAS, not VA, pays retired pay, so start pay questions here.

    Free

  • Concurrent Retirement and Disability Pay (CRDP) — DFAS↗

    Restores retired pay that was waived for VA compensation, for retirees rated 50% or more — in most cases DFAS applies it automatically from VA data; no application.

    Free

  • Combat-Related Special Compensation (CRSC) — DFAS↗

    Tax-free concurrent pay for combat-related disabilities rated 10%+, including many disability retirees CRDP excludes — but it is not automatic: you apply to your branch of service, and you can't draw CRDP and CRSC at once.

    Free

  • Survivor Benefit Plan (SBP) — DFAS↗

    The DoD annuity that continues a share of retired pay to a survivor, elected at retirement — and hard to change afterward. Note for survivors: the old SBP–DIC offset is gone (eliminated January 2023); SBP is taxable to the survivor, DIC is not.

    Free

  • Veterans burial allowance↗

    Reimbursement toward burial, funeral, plot and transportation costs (Form 21P-530EZ) — a partial reimbursement, not a direct payment, and non-service-connected claims carry a 2-year filing deadline; amounts adjust, so read the current figures on the page.

    Free

  • Eligibility for burial in a VA national cemetery↗

    The National Cemetery Administration's rule-set for veterans, spouses and dependents — the page to check before a family assumes, or is told, there's no eligibility.

    Free

  • Schedule a burial↗

    The at-need route runs through the National Cemetery Scheduling Office, not the cemetery itself — and a specific gravesite or cemetery can't be reserved in advance, which is exactly why the pre-need entry below exists.

    Free

  • Headstones, markers and medallions↗

    A free government headstone or marker for an unmarked grave anywhere in the world (Form 40-1330), or a medallion instead of one for a private headstone — send copies of discharge papers, never originals.

    Free

  • Presidential Memorial Certificates↗

    Free engraved certificates signed by the sitting President (Form 40-0247); they arrive automatically for national-cemetery burials — families using a private cemetery must apply, and multiple copies are allowed.

    Free

  • Burial flags↗

    One free U.S. flag per veteran to drape the casket or accompany the urn (Form 27-2008), via a funeral director, VA regional office or Post Office — by law there is only one, and VA cannot replace a lost one.

    Free

  • Military funeral honors↗

    Honors on request: at minimum two uniformed members, Taps, flag folding and presentation — arrange through the funeral director 48–72 hours ahead, and never pay a family fee for what DoD provides.

    Free

  • Pre-need eligibility for burial in a VA cemetery↗

    Get the eligibility decision while alive (Form 40-10007) so nobody is hunting for a DD214 at the worst moment — the decision doesn't guarantee a specific cemetery or gravesite, and VA re-validates at time of death.

    Free

  • Dependency and Indemnity Compensation (DIC)↗

    Tax-free monthly payments for surviving spouses, children and dependent parents after a service-connected or line-of-duty death (with the long-held-total-rating routes VA lists) — distinct from Survivors Pension and from SBP; VA considers DIC and Pension together and pays one.

    Free

    Needs-based monthly payments for qualifying surviving spouses and dependent children of wartime veterans, under congressional income and net-worth limits — unlike DIC, this one is means-tested, and the means test is what pension poachers exploit (see Fraud Watch).

    Free

  • Aid and Attendance and Housebound↗

    A higher pension rate — not a standalone program — when a pensioner needs help with daily activities, is bedridden or in a nursing home, or is substantially home-confined; you can't draw A&A and Housebound at the same time.

    Free

Lesser-Known Benefits

The big benefits get the brochures. These get found in bar talk a decade late: free passes, free tickets, base privileges from a 2020 law change, and local help. Local help is real, but it differs town by town, so it appears here as directories and checks, not as a list. Any site that prints “your city’s veteran perks” from one national template is making them up.

  • Military passes — America the Beautiful (NPS)↗

    Two different free passes, often confused: veterans and Gold Star Families get a free lifetime Military Pass; current service members and dependents get a free annual one. Both waive entrance and standard day-use fees at 2,000+ federal recreation sites. Bring ID or a DD214 to a staffed site; camping, tours and reservation fees are not covered.

    Free

  • Access Pass — America the Beautiful (NPS)↗

    The other lifetime pass: free for U.S. citizens and permanent residents with a medically documented permanent disability — a VA disability rating counts. Same entrance coverage as the military passes, and it is the one that may also cut some expanded amenity fees (camping, boat launch) by 50%.

    Free

  • Commissary, exchange and MWR privileges (VA)↗

    The 2020 expansion most veterans never heard about: any service-connected disability rating — not just 100% — plus Purple Heart recipients, former POWs and designated caregivers can shop commissaries, exchanges and MWR retail in person. No application; show a VHIC marked SERVICE CONNECTED, or a VA letter plus photo ID. Online exchange shopping: Shopping & Savings.

    Free

  • Vet Tix — Veteran Tickets Foundation↗

    Free tickets to sports, concerts, theater and family events, donated by teams, venues and promoters. A national 501(c)(3): currently serving members, honorably discharged veterans of all eras, and immediate family of those killed in action register after service verification. Tickets are free; a small delivery fee applies.

    Free

  • Blue Star Museums (NEA)↗

    Free museum admission each summer (Armed Forces Day–Labor Day) at 2,000+ museums — but read the scope: it covers currently serving members, including Guard and Reserve, plus up to five family members, with a CAC or dependent ID. Veterans and retirees are not included; the NEA's own advice is to ask the museum directly — many run their own veteran rates.

    Free

  • County Veteran Service Officers — NACVSO↗

    Your county employs an accredited service officer whose whole job is your benefits: claims help, state and local programs, and the perks no national list can publish. Free, local, and VA-recognized for claims representation; NACVSO's "Find a Service Officer" directory locates yours. This is also the fastest route to city and county perks where you actually live.

    Free

  • Stateside Legal↗

    Free civil legal help beyond the base legal office: a national directory of free and low-cost legal aid for veterans, service members and families — benefits, housing, family law, discharge issues — searchable by state or ZIP, run by Pine Tree Legal Assistance. For on-base help, see the legal assistance locator in Money & Debt.

    Free

  • Your state and county layer

    Many states waive state-park day-use fees or discount annual passes for veterans (terms vary wildly; some are free for disabled veterans), and public libraries commonly lend free museum and park passes. Neither perk can be listed nationally without inventing it: route through your state DVA for the park pass, and your library's pass program for the rest.

Print Your Own

Nobody is coming to put you on. This is the map — free.

People ask how money gets made — really made, not just the wins people post online. This guide answers in plain words. It shows how the money system creates new money, in the central banks’ own writing. “Printing money” has two halves — this guide keeps them apart

Half one: how the system prints. Under fiat money (money backed by a government’s word, not by gold), new money really is created out of thin air. Central banks create some. Regular banks create far more, every time they make a loan. That is not a rumor — the Bank of England says it in its own bulletin. See How Money Actually Works.

Half two: how a person “prints.” A person cannot make currency. What a person can do is build things that pay without selling an hour each time: rare skills, a share of a business (equity), things that pay rent or dividends (cash-flow assets), and savings that grow on their own growth (compound growth). Each one can also fail, and this guide names how. See The Honest “Printing” Playbook.

Outdated — do not rely on these

🧟 Old claims that are dead, retired, or were never true. Do not let an old video send you wrong:

  • The $25,000 pattern-day-trader rule is gone. FINRA (the group that polices U.S. stock brokers) replaced it with new same-day margin rules on June 4, 2026. Margin means trading with borrowed money. Firms may phase the new rules in until October 2027. Any guide still quoting the $25,000 minimum as current law is out of date.
  • The money-multiplier story is a myth. Banks do not take deposits and “multiply” them into loans. Loans create deposits. The St. Louis Fed’s own teaching series is titled R.I.P. Money Multiplier — see How Money Actually Works.
  • “90% of day traders lose money” is not an official number. No U.S. regulator publishes that rate. The regulators’ real words are strong enough — see Paper Before Money.
  • Project Gutenberg does not carry The Richest Man in Babylon. Many sites claim it does. It does not. The checked free edition is Global Grey’s, in Babylon.
  • “Guaranteed returns” is not a kind of investment. It is the shared warning sign of a scam pitch, per the FTC and the SEC. If it is guaranteed, it is lying.

The Honest "Printing" Playbook

Here is how a person can earn money that does not come only from trading hours for pay. Each idea also names how it can go wrong. Read both parts before you try any of them.

  • Print #1 — Sell a scarcer hour.

    Skills compound like money: a rarer, provable skill reprices every hour you sell afterward (Babylon's Cure 7). Highest return most people ever earn, lowest capital required — the cost is study time, paid up front.

    A play — steps you take, not a website

  • Print #2 — Own equity, not just wages.

    A business (or a share of one) pays its owners from other people's hours and from assets, not only from the owner's. The failure mode is symmetric: owners eat losses first, most new businesses fail, and equity in a dying business prints nothing. Start small; keep the day job until the numbers — not the dream — say otherwise.

    A play — steps you take, not a website

  • Print #3 — Buy cash-flow assets, slowly.

    Index funds, dividend payers, a rental you actually ran the numbers on: assets that pay while you sleep pay small and slow at first. Anyone showing you fast cash flow is showing you the top of a funnel — see Traps & Cons.

    A play — steps you take, not a website

  • Print #4 — Let compounding do the heavy lifting.

    The SEC's calculator (in Free Courses & Tools) is the whole trick: contributions + time + reinvestment. It cannot be rushed, which is exactly why it works and why it doesn't sell courses.

    A play — steps you take, not a website

  • Print #5 — Use credit as a tool, never as income.

    The system creates money by lending (How Money Actually Works); a person with strong credit borrows cheaper and can put borrowed capital into assets that out-earn the rate. The same leverage cuts the other way at the same speed: the asset can fall, the rate is owed regardless, and margin debt gets called at the worst moment. Rules that keep it a tool: borrow only against cash-flowing assets, never for consumption or speculation, keep payments survivable if income stops, and if you can't state the exit in one sentence, don't enter.

    A play — steps you take, not a website

  • The base rate nobody posts.

    The SEC: "Day traders typically suffer severe financial losses in their first months of trading, and many never graduate to profit-making status" (Paper Before Money). Building slowly has a boring base rate too — it's called most people who automate index investing and leave it alone do fine. Choose which base rate you want to live in.

    A play — steps you take, not a website

  • Stack the prints, in Babylon's order.

    Skills raise income → the surplus funds assets → assets throw off cash flow → cash flow buys more assets and cheaper credit. Each print feeds the next; skipping to the glamorous one (leverage, trading) without the ones beneath it is the standard way the story ends early.

    A play — steps you take, not a website

  • Position sizing is a convention, not a law.

    Rules like "risk 1–2% per trade" are practitioner folklore — no regulator sets or endorses a percentage (verified: FINRA and Investor.gov publish none). If you trade at all, treat such rules as seatbelts you chose, not physics — and the seatbelt's existence doesn't make the crash a strategy.

    A play — steps you take, not a website

Hold It Right — LLCs, Trusts & Keeping What's Yours

The rest of this list is getting and growing. This section is the keeping: the legal containers — companies and trusts that are not you — that hold what you build, so one lawsuit, one bad year, or one death doesn't take the whole pile with it. Honest limits up front, because this topic attracts more salesmanship than any other in the guide: no container here is a magic shield, every one carries a yearly fee or a paperwork cost, and the two cheapest protections in the section — insurance and basic estate papers — come before any filing.

  • Choose a business structure — U.S. Small Business Administration (SBA)↗

    Why entities exist, from the government's own small-business agency. An LLC (limited liability company — a business your state treats as legally separate from you) "protects you from personal liability in most instances": your vehicle, house and savings accounts won't be at risk if the LLC faces bankruptcy or lawsuits. Hold on to those two honest words — most instances. The rest of this section is about what makes an instance qualify.

    Free

  • Business Registration fees: Limited Liability Companies — NC Secretary of State↗

    The real price of the container, North Carolina as the worked example, straight from the state's fee schedule: forming an LLC costs $125 (the Articles of Organization — the short filing that creates it), and keeping it costs $200 every year (the annual report). Every state charges its own version of both numbers. Anyone quoting you thousands to "set up your LLC" is charging you to type your name into this form.

    Free

  • Get an Employer Identification Number (EIN) — IRS↗

    An EIN is your entity's tax ID number — the business version of a Social Security number — and banks ask for it to open the business account. The IRS's own words: "You can get an EIN for free directly from the IRS in minutes." It also warns: beware of websites that charge for an EIN — you never have to pay a fee for one. Lookalike sites charging hundreds for this free number are a standing scam (see the traps entry at the end of this section).

    Free

  • Two things make the LLC real: an operating agreement and separate money.

    (1) An operating agreement — your own written rules: who owns what, who can sign, what happens when an owner leaves or dies. The SBA notes some states can force an LLC to dissolve and re-form when membership changes unless an agreement is already in place. (2) A separate bank account in the LLC's name, under its own EIN. Business money in the business account, personal money in yours. This is not bookkeeping fussiness — the separation is the shield, as the next entry explains.

    A play — steps you take, not a website

  • The shield is conditional — mixing money ("commingling") is how it dies.

    Courts have a phrase, "piercing the veil," for ignoring the LLC and coming after the owner personally. It isn't automatic, and it isn't random: the classic way to earn it is treating the LLC's account as your own wallet — personal bills paid from business money, no separate records, the annual report never filed — until a judge decides the "separate" business was never separate at all. Run the entity like a real, separate thing from day one, and the separation is what a court sees. This is why the SBA says "most instances," not "always."

    A play — steps you take, not a website

  • Single Member Limited Liability Companies — IRS↗

    The tax truth that deflates a thousand sales pitches. To the IRS, a one-owner LLC is a "disregarded entity" — the tax man's words for "we treat it as if it isn't there." Its income lands on your personal return (Schedule C), taxed like any sole proprietor's, self-employment tax included. So: forming an LLC, by itself, saves no taxes. What it changes is legal liability, which lives in state law — not your tax bill, which lives in federal law. Anyone selling you an LLC as a tax trick is selling you a filing fee.

    Free

  • S Corporations — IRS↗

    The one real tax lever, with real strings attached. An eligible business can elect S-corporation tax treatment (Form 2553): profits pass through to the owners' personal returns instead of being taxed at the company level, and only the salary part carries payroll taxes — which is where the possible savings live. The strings: you must run actual payroll, file a separate return (Form 1120-S) every year, stay under 100 shareholders, one class of stock. Whether the savings beat the extra accounting cost depends entirely on your profit level. That calculation is the one thing a CPA genuinely earns their fee on — no honest list can run it for you.

    Free

  • Assets inside an entity vs. in your name — count the costs nobody mentions.

    Inside an LLC, a rental or business asset gets the liability separation — plus the overhead: the annual report every year, a registered agent (a name and address on file to receive legal papers), separate books, extra filings, and financing friction: lenders routinely make the owner personally guarantee an LLC's loan anyway, handing back at the signing table some of what the filing bought. In your own name, the same asset is simpler and cheaper to finance — and fully exposed. Neither answer is always wrong. The mistakes are paying entity costs to hold an asset with no real risk, and holding a genuinely risky asset bare. Run the numbers per asset, not per slogan.

    A play — steps you take, not a website

  • Living Trust Offers: How to Make Sure They're Trust-worthy — FTC (archived publication, Federal Depository Library)↗

    The revocable living trust, minus the seminar pitch. A trust is a legal container: a grantor (you) puts assets in, a trustee (also you, while you're able) manages them, beneficiaries receive them later. What it genuinely does: assets the trust actually owns skip probate — the court process that settles an estate, in public — and a successor trustee can step in if you become unable to manage things. What it does not do: because a revocable trust lets you change your mind and take everything back, it is no shield against your own creditors or lawsuits while you're alive, and it saves no taxes — the income is taxed to you just the same. The FTC's two standing warnings: a trust only works if it's funded — assets actually transferred into its name; an unfunded trust "can cost you money and endanger your best intentions" — and beware the high-pressure trust seminar and the mail-order kit.

    Free

  • Irrevocable trusts: a real shield with a real price.

    Move assets into a trust you cannot change or take back, and the separation becomes real — what is genuinely out of your hands is, in general, much harder for a creditor to reach. That is the entire trade, and there is no version without it: the protection is proportional to the control you permanently surrender, on documents signed in advance. These are precision instruments, with tax filings of their own, drafted by an estate attorney for one specific situation. Never a seminar product, never a DIY download. If a pitch makes an irrevocable trust sound like a revocable one with better marketing, walk away.

    A play — steps you take, not a website

  • What's an Umbrella Policy? — NAIC↗

    The cheapest protection in this section, and for most people the first one to buy. The NAIC is the state insurance regulators' own association. An umbrella policy is extra liability insurance stacked on top of your auto and home policies: when a claim blows past their limits, the umbrella pays the excess — legal defense costs included — and such policies are commonly sold in limits of $1 million or more. What it won't do: pay for damage to your own home or car, or cover deliberate wrongdoing. No state filing, no annual report, no attorney — an agent and a yearly premium.

    Free

  • The basic papers come before any entity.

    Three documents protect more families than every structure in this section combined: a will — who gets what, and who raises the children; without one, your state's default list decides both. A durable power of attorney — names who can pay your bills and handle your money if you can't; "durable" means it still works after your incapacity, which is exactly when it's needed. A healthcare directive — your medical wishes in writing, and who speaks them. None requires an entity or a filing fee, and all three are creatures of state law — use your state's official forms or a local attorney's flat fee, not a national kit with your state name pasted in.

    A play — steps you take, not a website

Paper Before Money

Paper trading means practice trading: fake money, real market prices. It teaches how buying and selling works, and how you react, with no real loss. Two honest limits. No official group endorses (publicly backs) any practice app. And practice feels easier than the real thing — fake trades fill kindly, and losing fake money does not hurt like losing your own.

  • Day Trading: Your Dollars at Risk — SEC↗

    The canonical warning, still live on sec.gov: "Day traders typically suffer severe financial losses in their first months of trading, and many never graduate to profit-making status" — and many lose everything and end in debt via margin. Read before any platform account, paper or real.

    Free

  • Rule 2270: Day-Trading Risk Disclosure Statement — FINRA↗

    The disclosure your broker must give you, verbatim from the rulebook: "You should be prepared to lose all of the funds that you use for day trading" — never retirement, emergency, or living-expense money.

    Free

  • Day Trading — SEC Answers↗

    The one-line official version: day trading "is extremely risky and can result in substantial financial losses in a very short period of time."

    Free

  • Paper-trade the plan you'd actually fund.

    Same instruments, same position sizes, same hours you could really keep — for months, not days. A paper system you'd never run with real money teaches nothing except the platform's buttons.

    A play — steps you take, not a website

  • Journal every trade, paper or real.

Meditation & Breathwork

A quiet corner of the internet. Simple ways to slow down, breathe easier, and feel a little more like yourself — all of them free.

No experience needed. No gear to buy. Nothing to believe. Just a few quiet minutes and your own breath.

This guide gathers the simplest, best-loved practices from teachers and researchers around the world: ways to sit, ways to breathe, and small habits of gratitude. Every technique here is free, and the free tools come before the paid ones. Take what helps. Leave the rest. Come back whenever you need a slower minute.

Three small skills — that's all this is

Meditation is resting your attention on one thing — your breath, your body, a kind word — and gently bringing it back when it wanders. That bringing-back is the whole exercise. Breathwork is using your breath on purpose: breathe slowly, and your body gets the message that it's safe to relax. Gratitude is noticing what's good, on purpose, for a minute or two. None of it takes talent. A few minutes a day is a real dose.

How to use this guide

  • Want a routine? A Simple Week gives you one small practice a day.
  • Sleep is the problem? Try the evening stack in A Simple Week, and the body scan in Ways to Meditate.

Start Here

  • Your first two minutes.

    Sit somewhere comfortable and set a timer for two minutes. Breathe normally, and rest your attention on the feeling of the breath — cool air coming in, warm air going out. When you notice you're thinking about something else (you will, within seconds), don't scold yourself. That's not failure; that's the rep. Gently come back to the breath. Repeat until the timer ends. That is meditation. Everything else in this guide is a variation on it.

    A play — steps you take, not a website

  • “Meditation doesn't work for me.”

    If you've tried and quit, it probably went like this: you sat down, tried to force your mind blank, and thirty seconds later you were grading yourself — and failing a test nobody was running. Hear this clearly: there is nothing to force. You can't force yourself to fall asleep either. You lie down, you get comfortable, and you let it happen. Same here. There's no score, no streak to protect, no finish line. When your mind wanders — it will — noticing that is the practice. The wandering mind isn't the problem. It's the workout.

    A play — steps you take, not a website

  • You don't have to clear your mind.

    Let that go early. A session where you notice your mind wandered twenty times is twenty gentle reps. Blank silence isn't the goal, and mostly it isn't on offer.

    A play — steps you take, not a website

  • A little, often.

    Five quiet minutes most days will do more than one long session on a Sunday. Small and steady is how every skill grows — this one included.

    A play — steps you take, not a website

  • Relaxation Techniques for Health — NCCIH↗

    A friendly page from the National Institutes of Health on the “relaxation response” — your body's built-in way of standing down: slower breathing, lower heart rate, loosened muscles. It covers deep breathing, guided imagery, and more, in plain language. A good read when you want the basics from a source that isn't selling anything.

Ways to Meditate

Many doors, same room. Try a few, and keep the ones that fit your life.

  • Focused attention — the basic.

    Pick one anchor: the breath at your nose, a count, a candle flame. Rest your attention on it. Your mind wanders. You notice. You return. That's all, and that's everything. Start with 5–10 minutes. When you catch a thought, label it silently — “thinking” — and go back to the anchor.

    A play — steps you take, not a website

  • Mindfulness / MBSR — Palouse Mindfulness (free online course)↗

    The most-taught program in the world, free online. Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction is an 8-week course built at the University of Massachusetts Medical School, and Palouse offers the whole thing — videos, readings, guided audio — with no fees and no sign-up. The practice: follow the breath, notice thoughts and body feelings without arguing with them, return. A real curriculum, at the price this guide prefers.

    Free

  • Body scan — UCLA Mindful Awareness Research Center (MARC)↗

    Attention walked slowly through the body, part by part — feet, legs, belly, chest, hands, face — feeling what's actually there. UCLA's center posts free guided body scans in English and Spanish, and its UCLA Mindful app is free too. Lovely before sleep, and a kind first practice if sitting still feels hard.

    Free

  • Open monitoring.

    Focused attention with the anchor taken away. Sit, and let whatever shows up — sounds, sensations, thoughts — arrive and leave without grabbing any of it. Start with two minutes of breath focus to settle, then open up. When you get hooked by a thought, that's the rep: unhook, and open up again. A good next step once the basic feels friendly.

    A play — steps you take, not a website

  • Loving-kindness (metta) — Galante et al. (2014)↗

    Deliberately wishing well. Silently repeat simple phrases — “may I be safe, may I be well” — first toward yourself, then a benefactor, a neutral person, and gradually everyone. It comes out of Buddhist practice; the lab version keeps the technique, not the doctrine. It can feel awkward at first, especially toward yourself. Awkward is a starting point, not a verdict.

    Free

  • Mantra.

    Sit comfortably, eyes closed, and repeat one word or sound silently — any word you like. Let thoughts come and go around it. Fifteen to twenty minutes is a classic dose, but five is a fine start. Simple, portable, quiet.

    A play — steps you take, not a website

  • Transcendental Meditation — course fee (official)↗

    The trademarked version of mantra practice: taught one-on-one by certified teachers over four sessions, with a personal mantra assigned. The honest price, from TM's own fee page: about $540–$980 depending on household income, $420 for full-time students. Some people genuinely do better with a teacher and a set system, and there's no shame in paying for one. Just know the basic technique — a quietly repeated word — is the free entry above.

    Paid

  • Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT) — Kuyken et al., JAMA Psychiatry (2016)↗

    Mindfulness training blended with cognitive-therapy tools, taught as an 8-week course. It's known for helping people who have had repeated depression stay well longer. This one is a program you take with a trained teacher, often alongside your own clinician — treat it as a course, not a solo practice, and it can be a real support.

    Free

  • Visualization / guided imagery.

    Close your eyes and build a calm place in detail — somewhere you feel safe — and walk through it slowly, senses first: what you see, hear, smell, feel. Or picture yourself calmly handling the thing you're dreading. Good for visual thinkers, and before anything that makes you nervous.

    A play — steps you take, not a website

  • Walking meditation.

    Meditation with the legs — for the days when sitting still simply isn't happening. Walk slowly — ten paces, turn, ten paces — with all your attention on the mechanics: lifting, moving, placing, the shift of weight. Ten minutes is a full session. A real practice, not a lesser one.

    A play — steps you take, not a website

  • Yoga — NCCIH↗

    Movement, breathing, and meditation woven into one practice. It began in India as a spiritual practice; the version in most gyms keeps the postures, the breath, and the meditation. Styles run from gentle to athletic — start gentle. The calm doesn't require the hard poses, whatever the photos suggest.

    Free

  • Tai chi & qigong — NCCIH↗

    Slow, flowing movements with controlled breathing and a settled mind. Tai chi began in China as a martial art before it became a health practice. It's easy on the joints, kind to balance, and a favorite for older adults — and for anyone who meditates better while moving. Qigong is its simpler cousin: fewer, repeated movements, same principles.

Breathing Exercises

Your breath is the one dial you can always reach. Slow it down, and your heart follows it down. Start with the gentle ones — they're the daily drivers.

  • Belly breathing — Ma et al., Frontiers in Psychology (2017)↗

    Learn this one first; every other pattern stands on it. One hand on your chest, one on your belly. Breathe in through your nose so the belly hand rises and the chest hand barely moves. Breathe out slowly through your mouth. Five minutes, a few times a day, until belly-first breathing is your default. You're retraining a habit of a lifetime — be patient with it.

    Free

  • Box breathing (4-4-4-4).

    Breathe in for 4, hold for 4, out for 4, hold empty for 4. Repeat for 1–5 minutes. A simple square of breath, popular with people who work under pressure. Use it before a hard conversation, a presentation, a tee shot — anywhere you need calm with your eyes open and your wits kept. Calm and sharp are not opposites.

    A play — steps you take, not a website

  • The sigh / cyclic sighing — Balban et al., Cell Reports Medicine (2023)↗

    The quickest mood lever in this guide. Two inhales through the nose — one full, then a second short one on top that tops the lungs right off — followed by one long, slow exhale through the mouth until empty. That's one sigh. Your body already does this on its own every few minutes and right before sleep; the technique is simply doing it on purpose. In a Stanford study, five minutes a day of sigh-style breathing for a month lifted people's mood. One to three sighs work in the moment, anywhere.

    Free

  • Coherent breathing, ~5.5 breaths a minute — Zaccaro et al., Frontiers in Human Neuroscience (2018)↗

    One steady, slow pace: roughly 5–6 counts in, 5–6 counts out, no holds — near five and a half breaths a minute, around the rate where your heart and breath settle into step with each other. Five to twenty minutes. If you build a daily habit on one pattern, this is a good one to build it on.

    Free

  • Extended exhale.

    The simplest calming rule there is: make the out-breath longer than the in-breath — in for 4, out for 6 or 8, no holds. Exhaling is the half of the cycle that slows the heart. No app, no count beyond that ratio. Once you've practiced it at your desk, you can use it mid-sentence in a meeting and nobody will know.

    A play — steps you take, not a website

  • Alternate-nostril breathing.

    From yoga's breath-control tradition. Sit tall. Close the right nostril with your thumb and breathe in through the left. Close both briefly, then release the right and breathe out through it. In through the right, close, out through the left. That's one round; do 5–10. What it reliably does is make you breathe slowly and on purpose — which is most of the benefit. Skip it if your nose is blocked; forcing it defeats the purpose.

    A play — steps you take, not a website

  • 4-7-8 breathing (the Relaxing Breath) — Dr. Andrew Weil↗

    Taught the way its source teaches it. Tip of the tongue on the ridge just behind your upper front teeth, kept there throughout. (1) Breathe out completely through the mouth with a whoosh. (2) Close the mouth, breathe in quietly through the nose to a count of 4. (3) Hold for 7. (4) Out through the mouth, whoosh, for 8. That's one cycle — do four cycles total, twice a day. The ratio is what matters, not the seconds — if the hold feels hard, speed the whole thing up evenly. Dr. Weil adapted it from pranayama, yoga's ancient breath control. A lovely lights-out ritual.

    Free

  • Strong breathwork (Wim Hof style).

    Rounds of fast, deep breathing, then holding the breath out on empty lungs — often paired with cold showers or cold water. It's the intense end of the pool, and some people swear by it. Two calm rules if you try it: only sitting or lying down, on dry land — and never in water, never before swimming, never while driving, because strong breathing can make you lightheaded, and that's fine on a couch and dangerous in a pool. If you're pregnant, or have heart, blood-pressure, or seizure concerns, check with a doctor first — or simply enjoy the gentle patterns above. They carry most of the benefit anyway.

    A play — steps you take, not a website

Gratitude

The smallest practice in this guide. A few minutes of noticing what's good, and who helped. Small, real, and worth having.

  • Counting blessings vs. burdens — Emmons & McCullough (2003)↗

    The founding studies of the field. People who listed things they were grateful for each week felt better about their lives than people who listed hassles or everyday events. The practice: once a week, write down up to five things you're grateful for — one line each on why.

    Free

  • Three good things & the gratitude visit — Seligman et al., American Psychologist (2005)↗

    Two practices from one well-known study, and both earned their place. Three good things: each night, write three things that went well and why — in the study, the lift lasted for months. The gratitude visit: write a thank-you letter to someone who changed your life, and deliver it in person — the biggest single boost of anything tested. The visit fades; the nightly habit lasts. Small and repeated beats big and once.

    Free

  • The gratitude letter, step by step — Greater Good in Action (UC Berkeley)↗

    The gratitude visit as a ready-to-run practice, from Berkeley's Greater Good Science Center. About 10 minutes to write, 30 for the visit. Pick someone you never properly thanked. Write directly to them, and be specific about what they did and what it changed — specific is what makes it land, for them and for you. Deliver it in person if you possibly can. Yes, it's awkward. Do it anyway.

    Free

  • Keep it fresh.

    Here's the classic way this practice goes stale: “coffee, bed, dog,” every day, until the list means nothing. The fixes are simple. Rotate the lens — a person, a place, a small pleasure, something that didn't go wrong. Go specific — not “my brother” but “my brother drove over with a ladder without being asked.” Include the why — what caused each good thing. A practice you keep beats a practice you perfect.

    A play — steps you take, not a website

  • Gratitude is not pretending.

    Nothing here asks you to call hard things good, deny that something hurt, or perform thankfulness for an audience. The practice is bookkeeping, not acting — the good entries go in the ledger next to the real ones, not on top of them. You can be grateful and angry at the same time. Honest people often are.

A Simple Week

One small thing a day. Nothing here takes more than ten minutes.

  • The 7-day starter plan.

    Day 1: the 2-minute starter practice from Start Here. Day 2: a 5-minute guided body scan (free recordings at UCLA MARC, in Free Tools). Day 3: sighs — 5 minutes of sigh-paced breathing, or one deliberate sigh each time stress spikes. Day 4: a 10-minute walking meditation. Day 5: three good things, written, with the why for each. Day 6: 5 minutes of coherent breathing, or 5 minutes of loving-kindness. Day 7: repeat your favorite from the week, at 10 minutes. Then keep the two or three that earned it. The rest will wait for you — they've waited a few thousand years already.

    A play — steps you take, not a website

  • The morning stack (10 minutes).

    Three sighs on waking → 5 minutes of focused attention → one line of gratitude, written or spoken, specific. Breath first, because it works before motivation wakes up. Gratitude last, because the day hasn't had a chance to argue yet.

    A play — steps you take, not a website

  • The workday reset (2 minutes, eyes open).

    Before a hard meeting: four rounds of box breathing. Mid-afternoon slump: stand up, walk, and run long exhales — in for 4, out for 8 — for one minute. Neither needs a closed door or an explanation. A practice you don't have to hide is a practice you'll keep.

    A play — steps you take, not a website

  • The evening stack (in bed).

    Three good things on paper or in a notes app. Then 4-7-8 breathing, four rounds, lights out. If your mind is still racing after ten minutes, put on a body scan recording and let someone else's voice do the pacing. There's no prize for white-knuckling the dark.

  • Anchor it to something.

    Attach the practice to something that already happens every day: after the coffee starts brewing, after the seatbelt clicks, after brushing your teeth. “After X, I do two minutes” survives busy weeks that kill “I'll meditate sometime today.” And when a day goes sideways, shrink the dose before you skip the day — two minutes counts.

  • “My mind won't sit still.”

    Good news: that is the workout. A wandering mind isn't a broken meditation — noticing the wander and returning is the exact rep. Practical fixes: shorten the session, open your eyes, count the exhales (counting gives the mind a railing), or move — walking meditation and tai chi count fully. There's no such thing as being bad at this. There's only dose and fit, and both can be adjusted.

Before You Buy Anything

Every technique in this guide is free. What's sold in this space is packaging — sometimes honest, sometimes not. Two minutes here can save you real money.

  • When a Business Offer or Coaching Program Is a Scam — FTC↗

    The Federal Trade Commission's page on coaching schemes lists the tells: guaranteed results, pressure to act now, testimonials in place of evidence, and requests for more money after you've already paid. Wellness gurus run the same script with better lighting — swap “income” for “transformation” and every line still scans. Once you've seen the script, you can't unsee it.

    Free

  • The funnel, in one line.

    Free video → free “masterclass” (a sales pitch with breathing breaks) → paid course → retreat → five-figure “certification.” At every step, the product is the next step. A good teacher's success is that you need them less over time. A funnel's success is that you never stop paying.

  • Big promises are the tell.

    “Detox your trauma in one session.” “Rewire your brain in 8 weeks.” “Ancient secret they don't want you to know.” If a claim sounds like magic, it isn't this. The real practices are quiet, gradual, and free — and they're all in this guide.

Gentle Notes

Four short notes, kept small on purpose. This guide is meant to lower your heart rate, not raise it.

  • Go at your own pace.

    Shorter is fine. Eyes open is fine. Stopping a session whenever you want is fine — you are always allowed to stop. Some days, sitting quietly will feel easy; some days it won't, and both are normal. This is rest, not a test.

    A play — steps you take, not a website

  • Strong breathing, two rules.

    Only sitting or lying down, on dry land. Never in water, never before swimming, never while driving. That's the whole safety section — everything gentle in this guide is fine anywhere, any time.

    A play — steps you take, not a website

  • A companion, not a replacement.

    These practices sit happily alongside a doctor or therapist — they don't replace one, and they're not meant to. If you're carrying something heavy, let a professional help you carry it. Asking for that help is a strength, not a detour.

    A play — steps you take, not a website

  • If you're ever in crisis.

    In the US, call or text 988 — the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline, free, day or night. That's the right tool for that moment, and reaching for it is the brave move.
